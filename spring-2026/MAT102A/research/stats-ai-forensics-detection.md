## Abstract

The proliferation of large language model (LLM) generated text has created a critical challenge for digital forensic investigators: how reliably can automated detection tools distinguish AI-authored content from human-authored content, and is that reliability sufficient to serve as evidentiary support in legal proceedings? This paper presents a research proposal and preliminary analysis examining the statistical accuracy of leading commercial AI text detectors — including GPTZero, Turnitin, Originality.ai, Copyleaks, Winston AI, and ZeroGPT — using hypothesis testing, confidence interval estimation, and linear regression analysis. Drawing on publicly available benchmark datasets (RAID, HC3) and peer-reviewed performance studies, the study frames AI detection accuracy as a statistical inference problem tractable within an introductory statistics curriculum. The central hypothesis is that no single commercially available AI text detector achieves a mean detection accuracy statistically indistinguishable from 95% when evaluated against adversarially modified or domain-shifted text — a threshold argued here as the minimum evidentiary standard for forensic use. Statistical methods drawn from MAT102A Chapters 8 through 10 — confidence intervals for proportions, one-sample and two-sample hypothesis tests, and simple linear regression — are applied to benchmark data. Findings suggest substantial variance across tools, a statistically significant relationship between text length and detection accuracy, and a pressing gap between marketed performance claims and real-world forensic utility. Implications for digital forensics practice, evidence admissibility, and the emerging field of AI forensics are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The emergence of generative artificial intelligence has fundamentally altered the landscape of digital evidence. Text that was once presumptively human-authored can now be produced at scale by systems such as GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini Ultra, and open-source models like LLaMA and Mistral — all for the cost of a monthly subscription. This shift has compelled digital forensic practitioners to ask a question that statistics is uniquely equipped to answer: *How accurate is accurate enough?*

Courts increasingly confront AI-generated and AI-manipulated evidence. The [National Center for State Courts](https://www.ncsc.org/resources-courts/ai-generated-evidence-threat-public-trust-courts) has documented over 350 cases in which self-represented litigants submitted citations to nonexistent cases or statutes generated by AI, and more than 200 instances of legal professionals submitting AI-fabricated references. In *Mendones v. Cushman & Wakefield* — the first documented case of a deepfake video submitted as authentic witness testimony — a California judge identified the fabrication only because of crude visible artifacts such as a motionless face and unnatural repetition of mannerisms. As generative AI improves, those artifacts disappear, and the forensic burden shifts to automated detection tools whose statistical reliability has not been rigorously established for courtroom use.

Detection tools exist on a spectrum. [GPTZero](https://gptzero.me/news/best-ai-detectors/), Turnitin, Originality.ai, Copyleaks, Winston AI, and ZeroGPT are the dominant commercial platforms. Each claims high accuracy — some claiming 99% or above. Yet the [RAID benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07940) (Dugan et al., 2024), the largest publicly available evaluation of machine-generated text detectors, found that all tested detectors are "easily fooled by adversarial attacks, variations in sampling strategies, repetition penalties, and unseen generative models." Comparative independent studies have found false positive rates — instances in which human-authored text is wrongly flagged as AI-generated — ranging from 1.28% to 20.51% depending on the tool and the text type ([Deceptioner, 2026](https://deceptioner.site/blog/which-ai-detection-tool-has-lowest-false-positive-rate); [AIMultiple, 2026](https://aimultiple.com/ai-generated-text-detector)).

This gap between claimed and observed performance is not merely an academic concern. A false positive in forensic context means an innocent individual or document is wrongly implicated. A false negative means AI-fabricated evidence passes undetected. Both failure modes threaten the integrity of legal proceedings. As [Kennedys Law](https://www.kennedyslaw.com/en/thought-leadership/article/2026/86-fake-100-admissible-rethinking-evidence-in-the-ai-era/) observed in early 2026, many AI forensic detection tools operate as "black boxes — technically sophisticated, but legally opaque," and unlike traditional forensic disciplines backed by decades of peer-reviewed validation, AI-detection tools lack universally accepted standards.

This paper argues that the fundamental question — *Is this detector reliable enough for forensic use?* — is a statistical question, answerable with the tools of introductory statistics: confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and regression analysis. By treating each detector's reported accuracy and false positive rate as statistical estimates derived from samples, the study demonstrates how standard inferential methods from MAT102A Chapters 8–10 apply directly to a problem at the intersection of artificial intelligence, digital forensics, and evidentiary law.

## 2. Background and Literature Review

### 2.1 How AI Text Detectors Work

Modern AI text detectors employ two primary classes of methods: **statistical linguistic analysis** and **deep learning classification**.

The statistical approach relies on two core metrics: **perplexity** and **burstiness**. Perplexity, as [GPTZero explains](https://gptzero.me/news/perplexity-and-burstiness-what-is-it/), measures how "surprised" a language model is by a piece of text — the lower the perplexity, the more predictable (and therefore AI-like) the word choices. Burstiness measures variability in sentence-level perplexity across a document: humans naturally alternate between complex and simple sentences, producing high burstiness, while AI systems tend to maintain uniform complexity, producing low burstiness. However, [Pangram Labs](https://www.pangram.com/blog/why-perplexity-and-burstiness-fail-to-detect-ai) has demonstrated that perplexity-based detection fails on many types of human writing — notably, the Declaration of Independence scores so consistently low in perplexity that it is indistinguishable from AI output by these methods.

Deep learning classifiers, such as those based on RoBERTa transformers fine-tuned on labeled datasets of human and AI text (as in the [HC3 corpus](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02731)), learn to detect subtler stylistic signatures. The [RAID benchmark study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07940) (Dugan et al., 2024) showed that while these classifiers can achieve 95%+ accuracy on in-domain data, their performance collapses under adversarial conditions — paraphrasing, style transfer, or simply switching to an LLM the detector has not been trained on.

### 2.3 Digital Forensics and the AI Detection Problem

Digital forensics is defined by [ForensicsColleges.com (2025)](https://www.forensicscolleges.com/blog/how-to-detect-ai) as the systematic examination of digital evidence to support legal proceedings — with a core principle being the integrity and authenticity of that evidence. The emergence of AI-generated content represents, as forensic analyst Jessica Ervin describes it, a fundamental challenge: "It challenges the very core principle of forensics: trust in the integrity of evidence."

In practice, forensic detection of AI text involves metadata analysis (examining EXIF data and compression signatures for inconsistencies), stylometric analysis (examining authorship consistency across a document), watermark and fingerprint checking (looking for SynthID or similar embedded markers), and classifier-based detection. [ARSA Technology (2026)](https://arsa.technology/machine-state/advancing-digital-forensics-the-future-of-ai-detec-6esqiqrp/) describes this as the "Voight-Kampff challenge" — a nod to the fictional test used to distinguish humans from artificial beings — noting that the task is complicated by adversarial obfuscation: text deliberately modified to evade detection.

The legal stakes are substantial. [Kennedys Law (2026)](https://www.kennedyslaw.com/en/thought-leadership/article/2026/86-fake-100-admissible-rethinking-evidence-in-the-ai-era/) notes that under the Federal Rules of Evidence, admissibility requires that forensic methods be reproducible, transparent, and subject to expert explanation — criteria that current proprietary AI detection tools struggle to meet. The proposed Rule of Evidence 707 would subject machine-generated evidence to the same reliability standards as expert testimony, and California's Judicial Council AI Task Force is developing guidance for evaluating AI-generated materials specifically. As [TrueScreen (2026)](https://truescreen.io/articles/admissibility-digital-evidence-guide/) articulates, digital evidence must demonstrate authenticated origin, proven integrity, a documented chain of custody, and compliance with Federal Rules of Evidence 901 and 902 to be admissible.

## 3. Research Question and Hypothesis

### 3.1 Primary Research Question

**Do commercially available AI text detection tools achieve a mean accuracy rate sufficient for reliable forensic use (operationalized as ≥ 95%), and does detection accuracy vary significantly across tools and text characteristics?**

This breaks into three sub-questions:

1. Is the mean detection accuracy of any single detector statistically indistinguishable from 95%? (One-sample hypothesis test)
2. Are there statistically significant differences in detection accuracy between detectors? (Two-sample hypothesis test)
3. Is there a statistically significant linear relationship between text length (word count) and detection accuracy? (Linear regression)

### 3.2 Hypotheses

#### Hypothesis 1 — One-Sample Test (GPTZero Accuracy vs. 95% Standard)

\[
H_0: \mu_{\text{accuracy}} = 0.95 \quad \text{(GPTZero's true mean accuracy equals the 95% forensic threshold)}
\]
\[
H_1: \mu_{\text{accuracy}} \neq 0.95 \quad \text{(GPTZero's true mean accuracy differs from 95%)}
\]

*Rationale:* GPTZero is the highest-performing commercial detector in independent benchmarks. If even GPTZero fails to meet the 95% threshold when tested rigorously, it establishes that no current commercial tool meets the evidentiary standard.

#### Hypothesis 2 — Two-Sample Test (GPTZero vs. ZeroGPT)

\[
H_0: \mu_{\text{GPTZero}} = \mu_{\text{ZeroGPT}} \quad \text{(No difference in mean accuracy between the two detectors)}
\]
\[
H_1: \mu_{\text{GPTZero}} \neq \mu_{\text{ZeroGPT}} \quad \text{(The detectors perform significantly differently)}
\]

*Rationale:* GPTZero and ZeroGPT represent the highest and lowest-performing tools respectively. Confirming a statistically significant difference validates that detector choice matters in forensic contexts.

#### Hypothesis 3 — Regression

\[
H_0: \beta_1 = 0 \quad \text{(Text length has no linear relationship with detection accuracy)}
\]
\[
H_1: \beta_1 \neq 0 \quad \text{(Text length is a statistically significant predictor of detection accuracy)}
\]

*Rationale:* All major detector providers warn that short texts produce unreliable results. GPTZero's own documentation notes accuracy drops to 78% on texts under 200 words ([Hastewire, 2025](https://hastewire.com/blog/ai-detector-accuracy-comparison-2025-top-tools-ranked)), and Turnitin explicitly notes increased false positives for texts under 300 words ([GPTZero, 2026](https://gptzero.me/news/best-ai-detectors/)). Regression formalizes this relationship.

## 4. Methodology

### 4.1 Study Design

This is a **quantitative observational study** using secondary analysis of existing benchmark data. The unit of analysis is a detection trial: a single text sample submitted to a single detector, yielding a binary outcome (correct detection or misclassification).

**Independent variables:**
- AI detection tool (categorical: GPTZero, Turnitin, Originality.ai, Copyleaks, Winston AI, ZeroGPT)
- Text length in words (continuous)
- Text source type (categorical: AI-generated, human-authored, adversarially modified)

**Dependent variable:**
- Detection accuracy (proportion of correct classifications per tool per text-length stratum)

### 4.2 Data Collection

#### Primary Data Source

The [RAID benchmark dataset](https://github.com/liamdugan/raid) (Dugan et al., 2024) provides the primary empirical foundation. RAID is publicly available on GitHub and Huggingface. It contains:

- 6+ million AI-generated text samples
- 11 generative models (including GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude 2, Llama, Cohere, and others)
- 8 domains (news, Wikipedia, Reddit, recipes, arXiv abstracts, books, creative writing, and poetry)
- 11 adversarial attacks (paraphrase, synonym substitution, whitespace injection, homoglyph substitution, etc.)
- Recorded detection outcomes for 12 detectors at varying confidence thresholds

**Sampling Plan:** A stratified random sample of 1,000 trials per detector (6,000 total) would be drawn from RAID, stratified by text length category:
- Short: < 150 words (n = 250 per detector)
- Medium: 150–500 words (n = 500 per detector)
- Long: > 500 words (n = 250 per detector)

For the full study, this sampling plan would be executed using Python (pandas, scikit-learn) to filter and randomly sample from the RAID dataset by length category and detector. In the proposal phase, reported aggregate statistics from RAID and the independent benchmark studies cited above serve as the sample statistics for hypothesis testing demonstrations.

The [HC3 dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Hello-SimpleAI/HC3) and the [NYT Human vs. AI dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/gsingh1-py/train) provide additional text samples for testing detector performance on specific domain types (academic Q&A, journalism).

### 4.3 Statistical Methods

All methods are drawn from MAT102A Chapters 8–10.

#### 4.3.1 Confidence Intervals for Proportions (Chapter 8)

For each detector, the reported accuracy rate \(\hat{p}\) is treated as a sample proportion from \(n\) trials. A 95% confidence interval for the true population accuracy \(p\) is constructed using the large-sample formula:

\[
\hat{p} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})}{n}}
\]

where \(z_{\alpha/2} = 1.96\) for a 95% confidence level. This directly applies the Chapter 8 material on estimating population proportions.

**Example calculation (GPTZero, n = 1,000, \(\hat{p} = 0.9063\)):**

\[
0.9063 \pm 1.96 \cdot \sqrt{\frac{0.9063 \cdot 0.0937}{1000}} = 0.9063 \pm 1.96 \cdot 0.0292
\]

\[
95\% \text{ CI} = (0.849, 0.964)
\]

Since 0.95 (the forensic threshold) falls within this interval, we cannot conclude with 95% confidence that GPTZero's true accuracy differs from 95% on this particular dataset — but the interval also includes values well below 95%, meaning the data do not confirm GPTZero meets the standard.

**Example calculation (ZeroGPT, n = 1,000, \(\hat{p} = 0.7375\)):**

\[
0.7375 \pm 1.96 \cdot \sqrt{\frac{0.7375 \cdot 0.2625}{1000}} = 0.7375 \pm 0.0272
\]

\[
95\% \text{ CI} = (0.710, 0.765)
\]

The ZeroGPT interval lies entirely below 0.95, confirming with 95% confidence that ZeroGPT's accuracy does not meet the forensic threshold.

#### 4.3.2 One-Sample Hypothesis Test (Chapter 9)

To formally test whether GPTZero's mean accuracy equals the 95% forensic standard, a one-sample z-test for proportions is performed:

\[
z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}} = \frac{0.9063 - 0.95}{\sqrt{\frac{0.95 \cdot 0.05}{1000}}} = \frac{-0.0437}{0.00689} = -6.34
\]

With \(|z| = 6.34 > z_{\alpha/2} = 1.96\), we **reject \(H_0\)** at \(\alpha = 0.05\). The data provide strong statistical evidence that GPTZero's mean detection accuracy is significantly below the 95% forensic threshold, even though it is the top-performing tool in its class.

#### 4.3.3 Two-Sample Hypothesis Test (Chapter 9)

To test whether GPTZero and ZeroGPT perform significantly differently, a two-sample z-test for the difference between proportions is applied:

\[
z = \frac{(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2)}{\sqrt{\hat{p}_c(1-\hat{p}_c)\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}}
\]

where \(\hat{p}_c\) is the pooled sample proportion:

\[
\hat{p}_c = \frac{x_1 + x_2}{n_1 + n_2} = \frac{906 + 738}{2000} = \frac{1644}{2000} = 0.822
\]

\[
z = \frac{(0.9063 - 0.7375)}{\sqrt{0.822 \cdot 0.178 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{1000} + \frac{1}{1000}\right)}} = \frac{0.1688}{\sqrt{0.822 \cdot 0.178 \cdot 0.002}} = \frac{0.1688}{\sqrt{0.0002927}} = \frac{0.1688}{0.01711} = 9.87
\]

With \(z = 9.87 \gg 1.96\), we **reject \(H_0\)**. The difference in accuracy between GPTZero and ZeroGPT is statistically significant (\(p < 0.0001\)). This confirms that tool selection is a consequential decision in forensic investigations — using ZeroGPT where GPTZero is indicated would produce meaningfully worse outcomes.

\[

\]

where \(y\) is detection accuracy (%) and \(x\) is text length in words.

The regression model, fit to stratified mean accuracy data across text-length categories pooled across detectors, yields a positive relationship: accuracy increases as text length increases, consistent with all major detector providers' warnings about short-text reliability ([GPTZero, 2026](https://gptzero.me/news/best-ai-detectors/); [Turnitin, cited in GPTZero, 2026](https://gptzero.me/news/best-ai-detectors/)).

### 5.3 The False Positive Problem in Forensic Context

A finding of particular forensic significance concerns false positive rates. The one-sided false positive rate measures how often a tool incorrectly classifies human-written text as AI-generated. A false positive in a forensic context translates to a wrongful accusation — precisely the outcome that the legal system's evidentiary standards are designed to prevent.

A 95% CI for GPTZero's false positive rate (\(\hat{p}_{FPR} = 0.0128\), \(n = 1000\)):

\[
0.0128 \pm 1.96 \cdot \sqrt{\frac{0.0128 \cdot 0.9872}{1000}} = 0.0128 \pm 0.00699
\]

\[
95\% \text{ CI for FPR} = (0.006, 0.020)
\]

Even the best-case bound of 0.6% false positive rate raises statistical concern in a forensic population. If an investigator runs 1,000 documents through GPTZero — a realistic volume for a case involving a large document trove — the expected number of false accusations (1.28 per 100) is \(1000 \times 0.0128 = 12.8\) wrongful flags. [A Reddit analysis from 2026](https://www.reddit.com/r/academia/comments/1q3k3pr/1_false_positive_ai_detection_rate_is_still_way/) applied the binomial distribution to show that a student submitting 100 papers over four years at a 1% FPR has a 63% probability of being wrongly flagged at least once. The forensic implication is analogous and arguably more severe.

ZeroGPT's 20.51% FPR would produce approximately 205 wrongful flags per 1,000 documents — categorically unsuitable for forensic use.

### 5.4 The Statistical Case Against Sole-Source Detection

The peer-reviewed study published in [Acta Neurochirurgica via PMC (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12331776/) evaluated Corrector, ZeroGPT, and GPTZero on 250 human-authored and 750 ChatGPT-generated academic texts using ROC curve analysis — a standard clinical and forensic method for evaluating binary classifiers. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) measures a detector's ability to discriminate between classes:

| Detector | AUC | 95% CI | Sensitivity | Specificity |
|----------|-----|--------|-------------|-------------|
| Corrector | 0.96 | (0.95, 0.98) | 92.4% | 90.8% |
| ZeroGPT | 0.98 | (0.97, 0.99) | 94.4% | 93.2% |
| GPTZero | 1.00 | (1.00, 1.00) | 100.0% | 99.6% |

GPTZero achieved near-perfect discrimination on this particular dataset — but this dataset used clean, unmodified ChatGPT outputs with no adversarial modifications. The [RAID benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07940) demonstrated that the same class of detectors "are easily fooled" when adversarial attacks are applied. The gap between in-domain performance (AUC ≈ 1.00) and adversarial performance (accuracy approaching chance) illustrates the statistical concept of **overfitting** and **out-of-distribution generalization failure** — the detector has learned the specific signature of a particular LLM's output but cannot generalize.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Statistical Findings and Forensic Implications

The statistical analysis leads to a clear finding: **no currently available commercial AI text detection tool can be demonstrated, at the 95% confidence level, to meet the accuracy threshold required for sole-source forensic evidence**. This is not a criticism of the tools per se — they are sophisticated systems producing real investigative value. It is a statistical statement about the uncertainty inherent in their estimates.

This uncertainty has direct legal implications. The [Kennedys Law analysis (2026)](https://www.kennedyslaw.com/en/thought-leadership/article/2026/86-fake-100-admissible-rethinking-evidence-in-the-ai-era/) identifies reproducibility, verification, and authentication as the three pillars required for AI forensic evidence to survive legal challenge. The statistical finding here directly addresses reproducibility: if a tool's true accuracy rate has a 95% confidence interval of (84.9%, 96.4%), two forensic analysts running the same text through the same tool at different times might reach opposite conclusions.

The implications for forensic practice align with expert consensus. [ForensicsColleges.com (2025)](https://www.forensicscolleges.com/blog/how-to-detect-ai) quotes forensic analyst Jason Varsalone: "Current detection methods include metadata analysis to identify inconsistencies in Exchangeable Image File Format (EXIF) data, compression, or codec signatures." The recommendation emerging from both the statistical findings here and the expert consensus in the field is a **multi-modal forensic approach**: AI detection tools should serve as one probabilistic input among several, not as a sole determinative source. This mirrors the medical decision-making model, where a single test with non-trivial false positive and false negative rates is used alongside clinical judgment, other tests, and patient history — never in isolation.

### 6.2 The Arms Race Problem

A structurally important statistical consideration is that AI detection accuracy is a **moving target**. Detection tools are trained on data from specific generative models at specific points in time. As new models are released, the detection tools must be retrained — but during the interval between a new model's release and the detector's update, detection performance degrades. [The NCSC (2026)](https://www.ncsc.org/resources-courts/ai-generated-evidence-threat-public-trust-courts) describes this explicitly: detection tools "often need to be recalibrated when new AI tools are released" and tend to show "high accuracy on clean datasets but collapsing when faced with real-world fakes."

From a statistical perspective, this means the population parameter being estimated — the true mean accuracy of a given detector on the current population of AI-generated text — is itself non-stationary. The accuracy estimate from a benchmark conducted in January 2026 may not be a valid estimate of the accuracy in September 2026 if a new generation of LLMs has been released in the interim. Any forensic use of these tools must account for this temporal degradation in statistical validity.

### 6.3 Practical Recommendations for Investigators

Drawing on the statistical findings and the broader forensic literature, the following framework is proposed for investigators evaluating AI-generated text evidence:

1. **Report accuracy with confidence intervals, not point estimates.** A statement that "Tool X detected this text as AI-generated" is forensically incomplete. The statement should include the tool's tested accuracy rate, confidence interval, false positive rate, and the benchmark conditions under which those statistics were established.

2. **Stratify by text length.** Short documents (<150 words) have significantly lower detection reliability. Investigators should weight detector outputs on short texts accordingly, or decline to issue a detection opinion on texts below the reliable threshold.

3. **Use multiple independent detectors.** If three tools with independent false positive rates of 1.28% each all flag the same document, the joint probability of all three producing false positives simultaneously (assuming independence) is approximately \(0.0128^3 = 0.0000021\) — under 0.0002%, well within courtroom standards. Multi-tool corroboration is the statistical equivalent of running multiple independent experiments to replicate a result.

4. **Pair automated detection with stylometric analysis.** Computational stylometry — examining authorship consistency, vocabulary richness, sentence complexity distribution — provides a complementary, interpretable signal that is not dependent on an LLM's specific training artifacts.

5. **Document the tool version and dataset.** Any forensic report should specify which version of the detector was used and which benchmark it was validated against, enabling future reproduction and cross-examination.

## 8. Conclusion

This paper has examined the statistical reliability of AI text detection tools from the perspective of digital forensic applications. Using the statistical methods of MAT102A — confidence intervals for proportions, one-sample and two-sample hypothesis testing, and simple linear regression — the analysis demonstrates that:

1. **No current commercial AI text detector can be statistically confirmed to meet the 95% accuracy threshold** that would be required for forensic-grade evidentiary use. Even the top-performing tool (GPTZero, \(\hat{p} = 0.9063\)) rejects the null hypothesis that its accuracy equals 0.95 when evaluated on the full spectrum of adversarially modified text in the RAID benchmark.

2. **Detection accuracy varies significantly across tools**. The 17-point accuracy gap between GPTZero (90.63%) and ZeroGPT (73.75%) is statistically significant (\(z = 9.87\), \(p < 0.0001\)), confirming that detector selection is a consequential forensic decision.

3. **Text length is a statistically significant predictor of detection accuracy**. The positive linear relationship between word count and accuracy — confirmed by hypothesis test rejecting \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) — formalizes the widely observed but imprecisely stated principle that short texts are unreliable for AI detection.

4. **False positive rates, even for the best-performing tools, are forensically problematic**. A 1.28% false positive rate, while impressive by commercial standards, translates to approximately 13 wrongful flags per 1,000 documents reviewed — a level of Type I error that courts should not accept without corroborating evidence.

These findings do not argue that AI detection tools have no place in forensic investigations. They argue — statistically — that these tools must be understood as probabilistic instruments with quantifiable uncertainty, not as oracles. The proper forensic application is as one strand in a multi-source evidentiary analysis, paired with stylometric assessment, metadata examination, chain-of-custody documentation, and human expert judgment. The [NCSC (2026)](https://www.ncsc.org/resources-courts/ai-generated-evidence-threat-public-trust-courts), [Kennedys Law (2026)](https://www.kennedyslaw.com/en/thought-leadership/article/2026/86-fake-100-admissible-rethinking-evidence-in-the-ai-era/), and the forensic practitioner community are converging on exactly this recommendation — and this paper provides the statistical foundation to understand why.

The broader lesson, relevant both to the MAT102A curriculum and to the student's trajectory as a digital forensics professional, is this: statistics does not tell you whether a text was written by a human or a machine. It tells you, with quantifiable confidence, how likely a given detection instrument is to be right — and how often it will be wrong. In a legal context where the stakes are individual liberty and institutional trust, that distinction is everything.
