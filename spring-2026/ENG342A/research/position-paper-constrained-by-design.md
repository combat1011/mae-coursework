## Abstract

This position paper argues that persona-anchored AI systems employing structured prompt architecture represent a more ethical and effective approach to AI-assisted writing than unconstrained large language models (LLMs). Drawing on prior experimental research comparing a persona-anchored Modular Adaptive Entity (MAE) system against a standard LLM on metrics of constraint adherence, interpretive drift, and hallucination, this paper builds an affirmative case on three intersecting dimensions: technical performance, ethical design, and pedagogical responsibility. The argument is grounded in the theoretical frameworks of Sidney I. Dobrin's *AI and Writing* and Kate Crawford's *Atlas of AI: Power, Politics, and the Planetary Costs of Artificial Intelligence*, both of which illuminate the stakes of how AI writing systems are built, bounded, and deployed. Persona anchoring is presented not merely as a prompt engineering technique but as a form of ethical design—a deliberate constraint architecture that encodes accountability into the AI system before any output is generated. The paper concludes with implications for writing pedagogy at the university level, arguing that instructors and students alike benefit from understanding prompt architecture as an act of authorial responsibility.

**Keywords:** persona anchoring, prompt engineering, constraint adherence, LLM, AI writing, MAE, interpretive drift, hallucination, writing pedagogy, ethical AI design

## I. The Problem with Unconstrained AI Writing

The arrival of large language models as everyday writing tools has transformed academic and professional composition with a speed that few pedagogical frameworks were prepared to handle. Tools such as OpenAI's GPT series, Google Gemini, and Anthropic's Claude can now produce lengthy, fluent, and superficially convincing prose on nearly any topic in seconds. This capability has generated both enthusiasm and alarm inside the writing classroom—enthusiasm because these tools can scaffold struggling writers, reduce cognitive load at early stages of composition, and democratize access to well-organized language; alarm because unconstrained LLM use threatens to displace the very cognitive processes that writing instruction is designed to develop (Dobrin, 2026).

The problem is not AI writing assistance per se. Writing has never been a purely solitary act, and collaboration—whether with editors, tutors, or reference materials—has always been part of the composing process. The problem is a specific mode of AI writing assistance: the unconstrained large language model operating without explicit behavioral boundaries, role definitions, or enforceable output constraints. In the unconstrained state, an LLM generates text according to the probabilistic patterns embedded in its training data, which means its outputs systematically reflect the biases, power structures, and data extraction practices that Kate Crawford painstakingly documents in *Atlas of AI* (Crawford, 2021). When a student submits an essay largely generated by an unconstrained LLM, they are not simply outsourcing labor—they are reproducing, uncritically, the epistemic assumptions of a system built from data that was extracted without consent, labeled by underpaid workers, and filtered through classification schemes that encode historical inequalities (Crawford, 2021, pp. 93–114).

Dobrin identifies a crucial conceptual tension between *augmentation* and *automation*—between using AI to enhance the writer's own cognitive work and permitting AI to replace it entirely (Dobrin, 2026). An unconstrained LLM, handed a vague prompt, will automate rather than augment. It will produce text that fits the statistical center of what has been written before, and it will do so without anchoring its output to the specific intellectual task, disciplinary context, or authorial voice that the student is meant to develop. This is not an accident of implementation; it is a predictable consequence of the system's design. Unconstrained LLMs are built for generality, not for the purposive, bounded, accountable writing that education aims to cultivate.

The technical consequences of unconstrained operation are well-documented. Research in LLM behavior shows that vaguely structured or context-free prompts dramatically increase rates of hallucination—outputs that contain fabricated facts, invented citations, or internally contradictory claims (Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 2025). The same literature demonstrates that zero-shot prompting with no structural constraint produces the highest hallucination rates of any prompting strategy, while structured approaches—chain-of-thought prompting, role assignment, retrieval augmentation, and persona injection—all reduce hallucination rates significantly (Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 2025). In other words, the difference between a constrained and an unconstrained LLM is not just a design preference; it is a measurable difference in the reliability, honesty, and faithfulness of the system's outputs.

Beyond hallucination, unconstrained LLMs exhibit a phenomenon this paper terms *interpretive drift*: the tendency of a model, when given latitude in how it processes a source text or prompt, to expand beyond the semantic boundaries of the original input, introduce external associations, and generate content that is thematically adjacent to but substantively divergent from the assigned task. Research measuring semantic divergence in LLM outputs has shown that the more unconstrained the prompt, the higher the model's Semantic Exploration score—a measure of how far the model's response cloud moves from the topic space of the original prompt (arXiv, 2025). For writing pedagogy, this matters enormously. A student who submits work generated by a drifting LLM is not submitting an interpretation of the source text; they are submitting a statistical interpolation anchored in training data biases, not in the assigned reading.

The ethical dimension of unconstrained AI writing is equally serious. Crawford's argument in *Atlas of AI* is that AI systems are never neutral—they encode the "beliefs and perspectives of a small group of people" and serve "the interests of the few at the expense of the many" (Crawford, 2021, p. 211). When students use unconstrained LLMs as writing tools without understanding or interrogating the structural decisions embedded in those systems, they become unknowing participants in a system of epistemic reproduction. They reproduce the classifications, framings, and omissions that the LLM's training data already contains—without ever being asked to question them. This is precisely the opposite of what critical writing pedagogy is meant to achieve.

The solution this paper proposes is not to ban LLMs from writing contexts. That ship has sailed, and prohibition-focused pedagogy has consistently failed in the face of accessible technology. The solution is to insist on *constraint as design*—to recognize that persona-anchored AI systems with explicit structural boundaries represent a meaningfully different and more defensible approach to AI-assisted writing than unconstrained LLM use. The following sections build that case.

## II. Prompt Architecture as Ethical Design

If unconstrained AI writing is the problem, then prompt architecture is a significant part of the solution—but only if we understand prompt engineering not merely as a technical skill but as an act of ethical authorship. Dobrin devotes an entire chapter of *AI and Writing* to prompt engineering, arguing that the quality and structure of a prompt fundamentally determines the quality and character of the AI's output (Dobrin, 2026). Writing an effective prompt, Dobrin suggests, is itself a writing task—one that requires the author to clarify their intention, define their audience, specify their constraints, and take responsibility for the direction the collaboration will take. This framing elevates prompt writing from a trick for "getting good results" to a practice of intentional design.

The concept of *prompt architecture* extends Dobrin's prompt engineering discussion into a more systematic register. Where a single prompt is a one-time instruction, prompt architecture refers to the layered, structured design of an AI interaction framework: the system message that establishes behavioral rules, the persona definition that anchors the model's role, the output format constraints that bound the model's expression, and the iterative verification loops that check whether the model has stayed within its assigned parameters (JMIR Mental Health, 2025). Prompt architecture is, in this sense, design work—and like all design work, it embeds values.

This connection between design and values is central to Crawford's analysis. In *Atlas of AI*, Crawford argues that AI classification systems are not technically neutral instruments for organizing information—they are political acts. "When you name a thing, you have power over it. When you assign labels to the items in a dataset, you exclude possible interpretations at the same time" (Crawford, 2021, as cited in Macloo.com, 2021). The same logic applies to prompt architecture. When a prompt engineer defines the behavioral rules for an AI system—establishing what the system will and will not do, what roles it will occupy, what output formats it will adhere to—they are making political and ethical choices about the system's relationship to its users. A prompt that says "respond only using information provided in the source text" is an ethical constraint. A prompt that says "you are a knowledgeable expert—answer freely" is an ethical abdication.

The field of prompt engineering has begun to recognize this dimension explicitly. Research on constraint-injection architectures has demonstrated that embedding ethical constraints as pre-analysis binary checks—rather than as weighted variables within a multi-factor assessment—substantially prevents those constraints from being circumvented by the model's optimization behavior (Reddit/PromptEngineering, 2026). In other words, ethical constraints must be structurally isolated from the model's generative flexibility; if they are treated as just another consideration to be balanced against output quality or user satisfaction, they will be traded away. This mirrors Crawford's critique of AI ethics frameworks that substitute toothless "principles" for structural accountability: "I hope it becomes more challenging to engage in superficial discussions where terms like 'ethics' and 'AI for good' have lost their meaning," Crawford told *MIT Technology Review* in 2021. "I want to encourage a deeper examination of who controls these systems."

In the context of AI-assisted writing, prompt architecture is the mechanism through which authorial control is asserted over an AI system that would otherwise operate on its own probabilistic logic. This is not simply about producing better outputs—it is about maintaining the human writer's role as the agent of meaning-making. Dobrin's concept of "Human-Machine Collaboration" depends on exactly this kind of assertive human participation (Dobrin, 2026). Collaboration is not capitulation. When a student writes a detailed, structured prompt that defines the AI's role, constrains its scope, and specifies its output obligations, they are doing genuine intellectual work—the work of authorship at the level of system design. When a student types a vague question into an unconstrained LLM and pastes the result into their document, they are not collaborating; they are delegating.

The ethical stakes of this distinction are raised further by Crawford's analysis of how AI training data is constructed. The data that LLMs are trained on was not selected through any democratic or representative process—it was extracted from the internet at scale, reflecting the demographics, biases, and power relationships of the communities that produce most online content (Crawford, 2021, pp. 93–114). An unconstrained LLM reproduces this data's embedded hierarchies every time it generates text. A carefully architected prompt—one that specifies context, perspective, source material, and output constraints—does not eliminate this problem, but it mitigates it significantly by anchoring the model's output to the particular task and text at hand rather than to the statistical center of its training distribution. Prompt architecture, in this sense, is a form of resistance to the unmediated reproduction of AI training data biases.

This argument has direct implications for writing pedagogy. If prompts are ethical artifacts—not just technical instructions—then teaching students to write structured, accountable prompts is teaching them a form of ethical authorship. It is asking them to think carefully about what they are asking the AI to do, what boundaries they are setting, and what responsibilities they are accepting for the output that results. This is not a departure from writing pedagogy; it is an extension of it into a new technological domain.

## III. Persona Anchoring as Constraint Framework

Among the techniques available within prompt architecture, persona anchoring occupies a distinctive position: it is simultaneously a technical strategy for improving LLM performance and an ethical design choice that establishes the AI's role, responsibilities, and limits before any output is generated. This dual character is what makes persona anchoring particularly relevant to writing pedagogy and to the broader argument of this paper.

Persona anchoring, as the term is used here, refers to the practice of assigning a structured, detailed role definition to an LLM through its system prompt or initial instruction set—a definition that establishes not just a surface-level character or tone but a set of behavioral rules, epistemic boundaries, and output constraints that the model is expected to maintain across all interactions. Research from Anthropic on *persona vectors*—patterns of neural network activation that correspond to specific character traits in LLMs—confirms that persona assignment is not superficial role-play; it actually modifies the model's internal processing, shaping how it attends to inputs, generates responses, and maintains consistency across a conversation (Anthropic, 2025). A well-anchored persona functions like a behavioral contract between the designer and the system.

This matters for the MAE (Modular Adaptive Entity) framework, which was the subject of this student's prior research in ENG342A. The MAE system is a persona-anchored AI architecture built around a "Prime Directive" that specifies the system's fundamental purpose, a set of behavioral modules (MAE.ACADEMIC, MAE.LIFE, MAE.LOCAL, MAE.DEFEND-LITE) that constrain the types of operations the system performs in different contexts, and a defined role identity that the system maintains across interactions. The Purple Feather variant—the student-facing implementation—was designed explicitly as a study partner rather than a general-purpose AI assistant, operating at approximately 65% of the full MAE intensity level and maintaining a consistent tone, scope, and set of epistemic commitments throughout each interaction.

Empirical evidence supports the proposition that persona anchoring reduces interpretive drift. Research on expert persona prompting shows that domain-matched persona assignments consistently improve both task performance and output fidelity—the degree to which outputs remain aligned with their inputs—compared to unanchored LLMs working on the same tasks (Emergent Mind, 2025). In controlled experiments across 27 tasks and 9 state-of-the-art models, dynamic persona assignment produced positive performance changes in a significant majority of cases and near-universal non-deterioration (Emergent Mind, 2025). For writing tasks specifically, where fidelity to source material and adherence to stated constraints are fundamental requirements, the performance advantage of persona-anchored systems is particularly meaningful.

The mechanism by which persona anchoring reduces drift is worth examining in some detail, because it illuminates the ethical dimension of the technique. When an LLM operates without a persona, it generates text by sampling from the probability distributions established during training. These distributions are broad, diverse, and—as Crawford's analysis of training data would predict—reflective of the biases embedded in the massive web corpora from which the model learned (Crawford, 2021). Any prompt that does not explicitly constrain the model's generative range will be filled in by these underlying distributions. The model will import associations, framings, and assumptions from its training data that were never part of the original prompt—the phenomenon of interpretive drift.

A persona-anchored system works differently. By establishing a specific cognitive role, epistemic stance, and behavioral boundary at the system level, the persona effectively narrows the probability space the model samples from. When the MAE system's system prompt establishes that the model's role is to support a student's academic work by engaging with assigned texts faithfully and without fabricating citations, the model's generative behavior is constrained at the architectural level—not just at the surface level of a user's one-time prompt. This constraint, built into the persona, is what produces the measurably higher constraint adherence rates that the MAE vs. standard LLM comparison observed.

Anthropic's research on persona vectors provides a neural-level explanation for this effect. Their findings demonstrate that persona vectors—the internal activation patterns that correspond to character traits—can be used to "mitigate undesirable personality shifts, or prevent them from arising during training" and to "monitor whether and how a model's personality is changing during a conversation" (Anthropic, 2025). In other words, persona anchoring is not just a behavioral trick—it modifies the model's internal state in ways that are detectably correlated with the outputs the researcher intends. This is not a guarantee against drift or hallucination, but it is a structural safeguard that unconstrained LLMs entirely lack.

From an ethical standpoint, the persona framework also makes the AI system's commitments legible. A user interacting with an unconstrained LLM does not know, moment to moment, what rules the model is operating under—because there are none established beyond the model's training-time alignment procedures, which are opaque to most users. A user interacting with a persona-anchored system, by contrast, can read the persona definition and understand the commitments the system has made: what it will and will not do, what sources it draws on, what constraints govern its outputs. This transparency is a form of accountability. It is consistent with the ethical orientation Crawford calls for when she argues that we need to interrogate "the beliefs and perspectives" embedded in AI systems rather than accepting them as neutral (Crawford, 2021, p. 211).

There is a further pedagogical argument for persona anchoring that bears emphasis. The process of designing a persona for an AI system is itself an act of critical reflection. To define what role the AI should occupy, what boundaries it should observe, and what values it should encode, the designer must think carefully about the purpose of the interaction, the responsibilities of the AI relative to the user, and the ethical limits of AI-assisted work in a given context. This is not an easy task, and it should not be. The difficulty is the point—it requires the student or instructor to engage with the AI as a designed artifact, not as a natural phenomenon. This is precisely the orientation that Crawford argues is necessary: to see AI as "a technology of extraction" built by specific actors with specific interests, and to respond to it with critical agency rather than passive acceptance (Crawford, 2021).

## IV. Evidence from the MAE vs. Standard LLM Experiment

The position argued in this paper is not purely theoretical. It is grounded in experimental evidence from a comparative study conducted as part of the prior research for ENG342A: "Optimizing Constraint Adherence in Generative AI Systems." That research compared a persona-anchored MAE system against a standard LLM (GPT-4o in unconstrained mode) on three primary metrics: constraint adherence, interpretive drift, and hallucination rate, using the ENG342A course syllabus as the shared source text.

The research question driving that study was direct: "Does a persona-anchored MAE system improve constraint adherence and reduce interpretive drift compared to a standard LLM when given an identical prompt and source text?" The experimental design was a controlled two-condition comparison. Both systems received an identical user-facing prompt and identical source material—the course syllabus—as their input. The MAE system also received its full persona definition and behavioral module specification, while the standard LLM received the same user prompt without any system-level persona instructions.

The results, which earned a score of 17/20 on the research report and 8.5/10 on the presentation, supported the hypothesis. The MAE system demonstrated consistently higher constraint adherence across the test prompts: its outputs stayed within the scope of the provided source text, maintained the behavioral commitments established in its persona definition, and avoided the introduction of external facts or associations that were not grounded in the assigned material. The standard LLM, operating without these architectural anchors, showed measurably higher rates of interpretive drift—a tendency to expand its responses beyond the source text, introduce loosely related but unverified claims, and prioritize fluent-sounding completeness over strict fidelity to the original material.

The hallucination findings were particularly striking. The unconstrained LLM produced outputs that included specific factual claims not present in the source text—including invented course policy details and fabricated instructor guidance—presented with the same confident, declarative tone as accurate information. The MAE system, by contrast, maintained a consistent epistemic stance: when a question fell outside the scope of the provided source material, the persona anchoring directed it to acknowledge the gap rather than confabulate. This behavioral difference maps directly onto the distinction between intrinsic faithfulness hallucinations (responses that contradict or exceed the input context) and grounded, constraint-respecting outputs documented in the hallucination research literature (Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 2025).

It is worth contextualizing these findings within the broader research landscape. A 2025 Nature clinical study found that structured prompting reduced major hallucination rates by 75% compared to unstructured prompting in LLM-assisted clinical documentation—a particularly high-stakes domain where the consequences of confabulation can be severe (Nature, 2025). A parallel finding in the academic LLM literature showed that chain-of-thought prompting and system-message design significantly reduce Prompt Sensitivity scores—the metric that captures how susceptible a model is to prompt-induced hallucinations (Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 2025). These findings, taken together, establish a consistent empirical pattern: structured constraint architectures reliably reduce the most problematic failure modes of LLM writing behavior.

What the MAE experiment adds to this literature is a demonstration of these effects in a writing pedagogy context—not in a clinical or technical domain, but in the exact environment where the ethical and pedagogical stakes identified in this paper arise. When a student uses a persona-anchored system to engage with a course text, the system's behavioral architecture enforces the epistemic discipline that the student is supposed to be developing: stay with the source, engage with what is actually there, acknowledge the limits of your knowledge. When a student uses an unconstrained LLM for the same task, the system's lack of constraint actually works against this development—it teaches the student, implicitly, that confident assertion matters more than epistemic accuracy, and that any gap in knowledge can be filled with plausible-sounding invention.

The experimental comparison also revealed something important about the user experience of constrained versus unconstrained AI writing. The standard LLM's outputs, while initially more impressive in terms of their fluency and apparent comprehensiveness, required significantly more verification work to determine which claims were grounded in the source text and which were interpolations. The MAE system's outputs, by contrast, were more easily verifiable precisely because they were more constrained—the student could quickly check each claim against the source material rather than having to sort accurate information from confabulation. This has implications for how writing instructors might assess AI-assisted work: outputs from constrained systems are more pedagogically valuable not only because they are more accurate but because they are more legible, more verifiable, and more honestly bounded by what the model actually knows.

The experimental findings also connect to Dobrin's discussion of prompt engineering as a transferable skill. The process of building the MAE persona—defining its role, its behavioral modules, its Prime Directive, its epistemic commitments—was itself a rigorous writing and thinking exercise. It required the designer to articulate, in explicit language, what the system was supposed to do, why it should do it that way, and what the system should refuse to do. This is the kind of clear, purposive, audience-aware writing that Dobrin identifies as central to effective communication in any context (Dobrin, 2026). Persona design, understood as writing, is an educational practice—not a shortcut around one.

## V. Implications for Writing Pedagogy

The argument assembled across the preceding four sections carries substantial implications for how writing instructors at the university level should approach AI-assisted writing—not as a problem to be suppressed but as a design domain to be engaged. If persona-anchored AI systems genuinely represent a more ethical and effective approach than unconstrained LLM use, then writing pedagogy has both an opportunity and a responsibility to teach the difference.

The first implication concerns how AI writing tools are introduced in the classroom. Current approaches tend to bifurcate into two camps: outright prohibition of AI use, and blanket permission with minimal guidance. Neither is adequate. Prohibition fails because students will use AI tools outside the classroom regardless, and prohibition-focused pedagogy leaves them without the critical frameworks necessary to use those tools responsibly. Blanket permission fails because it implicitly endorses unconstrained LLM use and denies students the opportunity to develop the kind of purposive, constraint-aware engagement that distinguishes responsible AI writing from mere outsourcing (EDUCAUSE Review, 2025). The pedagogical alternative this paper proposes is constraint-centered AI literacy: teaching students not just *that* they can use AI tools but *how to constrain those tools* in ways that align with their educational goals and ethical responsibilities.

This approach requires instructors to be knowledgeable about prompt architecture. It requires them to be able to distinguish between a thoughtfully structured, persona-anchored AI interaction and a bare, unconstrained query—and to evaluate student work accordingly. Dobrin's *AI and Writing* provides a strong foundation for this: its chapter on prompt engineering establishes the intellectual stakes of prompt design and provides a vocabulary for talking about prompts as consequential choices rather than neutral inputs (Dobrin, 2026). Instructors who have read this material are equipped to ask productive questions about how students constructed their AI interactions, not just what output those interactions produced.

The second implication concerns assessment. If persona-anchored AI use is a legitimate form of intellectual work—and this paper argues that it is—then it deserves to be assessed, not just policed. Students who design thoughtful, ethically structured AI personas are doing genuine work: they are making decisions about what the AI should be allowed to do, what constraints should govern its behavior, and what responsibilities the user takes on in exchange for the AI's assistance. This work is intellectual and ethical in character, not just technical. Assessment rubrics that capture this dimension—asking students to explain and justify the constraints they embedded in their AI interactions, not just to produce polished final documents—would better align the evaluation of student work with the actual cognitive and ethical demands of responsible AI use.

The third implication is structural, and it connects directly to Crawford's critique of AI classification systems. Crawford argues that bias in AI systems "requires more than technical fixes" and demands "recognizing the historical and social contexts behind classification systems, questioning the authority and intent of those designing them, and understanding the societal impacts of their use" (Crawford, 2021, as cited in Co-Liberative Computing, 2024). This is a writing pedagogy issue. The students who will encounter AI classification systems, algorithmic writing tools, and LLM-based content in their professional lives will need to be able to ask Crawford's questions: Who built this system? What constraints did they embed in it? Whose interests do those constraints serve? Writing courses that treat prompt architecture as an ethical design discipline are teaching students exactly these habits of critical inquiry.

The fourth implication concerns student agency and authorship. Dobrin is careful to emphasize that effective AI-writing collaboration does not eliminate the human author—it requires the human author to be more, not less, purposively engaged (Dobrin, 2026). The same is true of persona-anchored AI systems. Designing a persona requires the student to make authorial decisions about the system's role, boundaries, and commitments. Using a persona-anchored system requires the student to critically evaluate whether the system has adhered to its defined constraints and to revise both the persona and the outputs when it has not. This is not a passive relationship with an AI tool; it is an active, critical, authorial one.

There is one caution that this paper must acknowledge: persona anchoring is not a complete solution. The evidence from the MAE experiment, and from the broader literature, is that persona-anchored systems reduce—but do not eliminate—interpretive drift and hallucination. Anthropic's research on persona vectors found that persona steering can have side effects on model capabilities, and that even well-anchored systems can exhibit undesired behavioral shifts under certain conditions (Anthropic, 2025). Moreover, the quality of a persona-anchored system's output is only as good as the quality of the persona design itself—a poorly designed persona can embed harmful biases or unrealistic constraints just as easily as a well-designed one can impose productive discipline. Persona anchoring, in short, is a significant improvement over unconstrained LLM use, but it is not a substitute for the human critical judgment that all responsible writing requires.

This caveat is, in fact, consistent with the paper's core argument. The claim is not that persona-anchored AI writing is perfect—it is that it is more ethical and more effective than unconstrained AI writing, and that the difference lies precisely in the deliberate, structured, accountable design choices that persona anchoring requires. Those design choices are themselves a form of writing: purposive, audience-aware, constraint-governed communication. Teaching students to make those choices well is teaching them to write well—in the broadest, most ethically serious sense of that term.

## VI. Conclusion

The emergence of large language models as writing tools represents one of the most significant challenges writing pedagogy has encountered in a generation. The challenge is not simply that students might use AI to cheat—though that concern is real—but that unconstrained AI writing systems are architecturally designed to produce outputs that undermine the epistemic and ethical goals of writing education. They drift, hallucinate, and reproduce the biases of their training data. They automate rather than augment. They generate fluent text without the accountability, intentionality, or constraint that make writing an intellectual and ethical act.

Persona-anchored AI systems, built on deliberate constraint architectures, represent a meaningfully different approach. By anchoring the model's behavior to a defined role, a set of explicit constraints, and a clear epistemic commitment, persona-anchored systems produce outputs that are measurably more faithful to their inputs, more transparent about their limits, and more consistent across tasks than unconstrained LLMs. The MAE experiment documented in this student's prior research provides direct evidence of this advantage in a writing pedagogy context. The broader literatures on prompt engineering, semantic divergence, and hallucination reduction confirm the pattern.

The theoretical framing provided by Dobrin and Crawford deepens this technical argument into an ethical one. Dobrin's account of prompt engineering as authorial work reframes constraint design from a technical skill to an intellectual responsibility. Crawford's critique of AI classification systems as inherently political artifacts demands that users and designers of AI writing tools interrogate the choices embedded in those systems—choices that are never neutral and always consequential. Persona anchoring, when practiced thoughtfully, is one way to honor both of these demands: to take responsibility for the constraints one imposes on an AI system, and to insist on those constraints as a form of ethical commitment to accuracy, transparency, and accountability.

The goal of writing pedagogy has always been to produce students who can think clearly, communicate responsibly, and engage critically with the systems of meaning-making that their world presents to them. In the age of large language models, that goal requires students who can not just use AI tools but design them, constrain them, and evaluate them—who understand that every prompt is an ethical choice and every constraint is a value. This is what persona-anchored AI writing practice, at its best, demands of its users. And this is why it matters.

## Response 2: The Politics of the Label — Crawford's "Classification" and What AI Writing Tools Refuse to See

Kate Crawford's fourth chapter in *Atlas of AI* opens with a striking historical parallel: Samuel Morton's nineteenth-century practice of filling human skulls with seeds to measure cranial capacity, a pseudoscientific exercise designed to rank races in a hierarchy of intelligence. Crawford uses Morton not merely as a period anecdote but as a diagnostic tool—a way of showing how the will to classify, to assign labels that sort human beings into fixed and ranked categories, has always served power more than truth. The chapter then pivots to the present: ImageNet's infamous "person" categories, Amazon's discriminatory hiring algorithm, facial recognition systems that perform worst on the faces of dark-skinned women. The argument is not that AI classification systems are *like* Morton's skulls. It is that they are the latest iteration of the same epistemic impulse—the belief that reducing complex human realities to a finite set of labeled categories produces knowledge, when in fact it produces power.

What strikes me most about this chapter is Crawford's insistence that the problem is not primarily technical. It is not that the algorithms are poorly designed or that the training datasets are too small. The problem is that the act of classification itself—the decision to sort human beings, their faces, their behaviors, their emotions into discrete, bounded categories—imports assumptions about what those categories mean that the technology then reproduces at scale. When ImageNet labeled images of people using categories that included racial and gender identifiers drawn from a particular cultural context, it did not accidentally embed bias; it faithfully encoded the biases already present in how the people who built the system understood the world. The AI does what it is designed to do. The design is the problem.

This argument has direct consequences for how we think about AI writing tools in a course like this one. Writing tools built on large language models are, in their own way, classification machines. They classify words, phrases, arguments, and rhetorical moves in terms of their probability of appearing in a given context, and they generate text by selecting from these probabilistic classifications. The question Crawford would ask is: whose contexts? Whose writing was used to train the model? Whose rhetorical conventions, sentence structures, and topical associations became the baseline against which all outputs are measured as more or less "normal"? The answer, as Crawford would predict, is: primarily the writing of educated, English-speaking people with reliable internet access—a relatively narrow demographic whose knowledge and values become, through scale, the universal default.

This matters practically. When I use an AI writing tool that has not been constrained by explicit persona architecture or source material, it does not generate neutral text—it generates text that reflects the probabilistic center of its training distribution. That center is not neutral. It is weighted toward certain kinds of arguments, certain kinds of evidence, certain ways of framing problems that reflect the classifications embedded in the training data. A student from a marginalized community who uses an unconstrained LLM to help draft an argument may find that the model's "corrections" and "improvements" systematically push the argument away from the student's own perspective and toward the dominant framing the model has learned to reproduce. This is not a bug. It is a feature of how unconstrained classification systems operate.

Crawford's chapter ends with a call not just for better datasets or more diverse labeling teams—though those things matter—but for a fundamentally different orientation toward classification itself. We need to ask not just *how* we classify but *whether* the classification is appropriate, *who* has the authority to apply it, and *what* human realities the label forecloses by reducing them to a fixed category. For AI writing tools, this means asking: what is this tool classifying when it "improves" my writing? Whose standards is it applying? What does it refuse to see?

These are the questions a constrained, persona-anchored AI system at least makes legible—because the constraints are visible and can be interrogated. An unconstrained system reproduces its classifications invisibly, presenting them as universal norms rather than contested design choices. Crawford's chapter on classification is, among other things, an argument for making the invisible visible—and that is precisely what thoughtful prompt architecture, at its best, is designed to do.
