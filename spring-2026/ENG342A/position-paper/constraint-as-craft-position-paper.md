# PART 1: POSITION PAPER

## Constraint as Craft: Persona-Anchored Prompting and the Future of Ethical AI Writing

---

### Introduction

Artificial intelligence has entered the writing classroom — not as a future threat but as an immediate and largely unmanaged presence. Students now compose alongside AI tools, and instructors are scrambling between prohibition and uncritical permission. Neither extreme addresses the real problem: not the existence of AI writing assistance, but the absence of structure governing how it operates. Unconstrained language models generate plausible text with no accountability to source material, no fidelity to rhetorical purpose, and no mechanism for tracing the reasoning behind what they produce. In a context where accuracy, coherence, and authorial intention matter, this is not a minor inconvenience — it is a structural failure.

Persona-anchored prompting systems represent a more ethical and effective framework for AI-assisted writing than unconstrained language models, because constraint is not a limitation on intelligence — it is the structure that makes AI-assisted writing accountable, reproducible, and pedagogically sound. By embedding defined parameters, behavioral constraints, and source-anchored instructions into the prompting architecture before generation begins, these systems transform AI collaboration from passive consumption into active design.

---

### The Problem with Unconstrained Models

The most well-documented failure mode of unconstrained large language models (LLMs) is hallucination — the confident generation of factually inaccurate content that is statistically plausible but empirically false. Research published in *Nature* confirmed that LLMs produce semantically inconsistent outputs across repeated identical prompts because they prioritize plausible-sounding text over verified claims (Farquhar et al., 2024, p. 625). Hallucinations arise structurally from training procedures that reward confident outputs over acknowledged uncertainty, optimizing models to perform certainty rather than accuracy (Zhang, 2025).

Kate Crawford's *Atlas of AI* (2021) extends this critique to the structural conditions that produce unaccountable AI behavior. Crawford argues that systems trained on vast, unattributed data corpora reproduce patterns without preserving the contexts, intentions, or reliability of their sources (Crawford, 2021). Unconstrained AI outputs carry no traceable lineage: they synthesize without sourcing and assert without grounding. Interpretive drift compounds the problem — without persistent anchoring, a model given the same task twice produces meaningfully different outputs, making accountability structurally impossible.

---

### Prompt Architecture as Authorial Work

Sidney Dobrin's *AI and Writing* (2023) offers a crucial reframe for what it means to work responsibly with generative AI. Dobrin argues that prompt design is not incidental to writing — it is a form of writing practice in its own right. The choices a writer makes when constructing a prompt — what persona to assign, what constraints to specify, what source material to include, what output format to require — are rhetorical choices that determine what kind of thinking the AI will perform (Dobrin, 2023). Giray (2023) extends this argument, contending that prompt engineering is an emerging competency requiring understanding of audience, context, purpose, and constraint — all classical rhetorical concerns (Giray, 2023). A writer who designs a persona-anchored prompt specifying source fidelity, tonal register, and structural scope has done real intellectual work before a word of output is generated. Designing the architecture of an AI interaction is authorship. The question is whether we teach it as such.

---

### Evidence from the MAE Experiment

The argument for persona-anchored prompting is not only theoretical. In an N=20 comparative experiment conducted as part of prior coursework in this course, the author compared outputs generated by a standard LLM (10 trials) against outputs generated through a persona-anchored Modular Adaptive Entity (MAE) architecture (10 trials), using the ENG 342 course syllabus as the shared source text and applying identical prompts across both conditions. The MAE-condition outputs demonstrated stronger constraint adherence — instructions specifying tone, scope, length, and source fidelity were followed at a higher rate. Interpretive drift was substantially reduced, and hallucination rates were lower. The trade-off was increased structural rigidity; MAE outputs were less stylistically variable but more reliably accurate. In an academic context, this is not a trade-off. Reproducibility and source fidelity are the relevant success criteria.

Nguyen et al. (2024) found corroborating evidence at scale: doctoral students who engaged iteratively with AI writing tools — adjusting prompts, specifying constraints, actively directing AI behavior — consistently achieved better writing performance than students who used AI as a passive resource (Nguyen et al., 2024). Structured engagement produces structured outcomes.

---

### Pedagogical Implications: Designing AI Interactions, Not Consuming AI Outputs

If structured prompting produces more accountable and academically useful AI outputs than unconstrained use, writing programs should teach prompt architecture as a core competency. EDUCAUSE's framework for AI literacy in higher education defines the relevant skill set not as familiarity with AI tools but as the capacity to critically evaluate, ethically apply, and actively direct those tools toward defined purposes (EDUCAUSE, 2024). That is precisely what prompt architecture requires.

The alternative — banning AI or leaving it unconstrained — produces worse outcomes. Prohibition drives AI use underground. Unconstrained permission leaves students dependent on outputs they cannot evaluate or account for. Teaching prompt design as authorial practice gives students agency: it positions them as architects of the writing process rather than consumers of its outputs. The Writing Across the Curriculum Association's 2025 statement on AI argues that writing instruction must develop students' capacity for rhetorical judgment and reflection when working with AI, not merely their ability to generate text (WAC Association, 2025). Prompt architecture is how that capacity is developed.

---

### Representing and Refuting the Opposition

The most frequently voiced objection to constraint-based AI prompting is that prescribing tone, structure, and scope eliminates the creative variability that makes language models valuable. This concern deserves a direct response.

Constraints do not eliminate creativity — they channel it. Research on creativity and constraint has consistently shown that focused constraints, which define parameters rather than merely prohibit options, enhance creative output rather than diminish it (Tromp & Baer, 2022). Poets have always worked within fixed forms; sonnet writers are not less creative than free-verse poets. Structure provides a generative frame within which unexpected ideas emerge. The same principle applies to AI prompting: a well-designed persona-anchored prompt produces output that is reliably on-task and source-faithful while still allowing substantive generation within those parameters.

In academic writing, the relevant criterion is not creative surprise — it is accountability. An AI output that is stylistically unpredictable but factually unreliable is worse than one that is structurally consistent and source-faithful. The MAE experiment confirmed this: persona-anchored outputs were more reliable and more academically useful. The opposition confuses creative freedom with epistemic irresponsibility. In writing pedagogy, accountability is not a constraint on quality — it is its precondition.

---

### Conclusion

AI writing assistance is not going away. The question confronting writing programs is not whether students will use AI but whether they will use it in ways that are accountable, traceable, and pedagogically sound. Unconstrained language models fail this test structurally: they hallucinate, drift, and produce outputs with no mechanism for accountability to source, purpose, or argument. Persona-anchored prompting systems succeed where unconstrained models fail because they encode the rhetorical decisions that define responsible writing at the level of architecture rather than leaving them to chance.

Constraint is not a limitation on intelligence. It is the structure that makes AI-assisted writing accountable, reproducible, and pedagogically sound. Writing programs should act on this accordingly: prompt architecture and AI constraint design belong in the writing curriculum as core competencies — not technical skills for computer science students, but rhetorical practices for writers. The intellectual work of designing a good AI interaction is the intellectual work of writing. It is time to teach it that way.
