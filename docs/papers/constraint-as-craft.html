Part 1: Position Paper

Introduction

Artificial intelligence has rapidly transformed writing from a solitary act into a collaborative process between human and machine. While large language models offer unprecedented generative capabilities, their unconstrained nature raises critical concerns about authorship, accountability, and ethical use in academic contexts. As Dobrin (2023) observes, generative AI programs "cannot currently discern between correct and incorrect information," making the structure of human-AI interaction a defining factor in output quality (p. 10). As students increasingly rely on AI tools, the question is no longer whether AI should be used in writing, but how it should be structured.

This paper argues that persona-anchored prompting systems provide a more ethical and effective framework for AI-assisted writing than unconstrained models. Constraint, often misunderstood as a limitation, is in fact the mechanism that enables accountability, reproducibility, and pedagogical value in AI-supported composition.

1. Constraint as a Foundation for Accountability

Unconstrained AI systems generate content based on probabilistic patterns without inherent responsibility for accuracy or intent (Crawford, 2021). This creates ambiguity around authorship and accountability. When a student submits AI-assisted work, it becomes difficult to determine: who is responsible for the ideas, how the output was generated, and whether the process aligns with academic integrity. As Crawford (2021) argues in Atlas of AI, these systems are "expressions of power that emerge from wider economic and political forces," operating without built-in mechanisms for transparency or user control (p. 8).

Persona-anchored prompting introduces structured roles and behavioral boundaries. By defining the AI as a specific persona with constraints, the system produces traceable and explainable outputs. This allows both students and instructors to understand the decision-making framework behind the generated content.

Constraint, therefore, transforms AI from an opaque generator into a transparent collaborator.

2. Reproducibility and Academic Integrity

A major issue with AI-assisted writing is the lack of reproducibility. The same prompt can produce different outputs, making it difficult to verify originality or process. Research on AI hallucination demonstrates that unconstrained models fabricate citations, generate false data, and produce outputs that appear correct but are factually unreliable (Farquhar et al., 2024). In academic contexts, this inconsistency undermines the integrity of submitted work.

Persona-anchored systems reduce this variability by: enforcing consistent tone and logic, structuring responses through predefined rules, and stabilizing output across iterations. Nguyen et al. (2024) found that doctoral students who engaged in iterative, highly interactive processes with AI tools — structuring and constraining their interactions — achieved significantly better writing performance than those who used AI passively as a supplementary source.

This mirrors scientific reproducibility. Just as experiments require controlled conditions, AI-assisted writing benefits from controlled prompting environments. Constraint becomes a methodological tool, not a restriction. In academic settings, this allows instructors to evaluate not only the final product, but the process of composition.

3. Pedagogical Value: Teaching Structure, Not Dependence

Critics argue that AI tools risk replacing student thinking. However, this concern applies primarily to unconstrained use. As Dobrin (2023) emphasizes, prompt engineering is itself a form of writing practice — designing the constraints under which AI operates requires the same rhetorical awareness, audience analysis, and structural thinking that composition courses teach.

Persona-anchored prompting shifts the focus from "generate the answer" to "structure the thinking process." Students must: define the role of the AI, guide its behavior, and evaluate and refine outputs. Giray (2023) argues that prompt engineering for academic writers requires understanding task decomposition, constraint specification, and iterative refinement — skills that directly reinforce writing pedagogy.

This encourages metacognition, where students think about how they think. Instead of outsourcing cognition, they learn to architect it. Constraint, in this context, becomes a teaching mechanism that reinforces writing as a structured process.

4. Ethical Safeguards Through Embedded Limits

Unconstrained AI systems can produce misleading, biased, or inappropriate content. Crawford (2021) documents how AI classification systems encode existing power structures, reproducing bias through the data they are trained on. Without guardrails, students may unknowingly submit flawed or unethical material.

Persona-anchored systems embed safeguards such as: tone restrictions, content boundaries, and reasoning transparency. These constraints act as ethical filters, reducing the likelihood of harmful or academically dishonest output. Structured prompting has been shown to reduce hallucination susceptibility by up to 60% compared to vague, unconstrained prompts (Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, 2025).

Rather than relying on external enforcement — plagiarism detection, policy violations — constraint builds ethics into the generation process itself.

5. Cognitive Load and Efficiency

Writing is cognitively demanding. AI tools can reduce this burden, but unconstrained models often create new problems: irrelevant output, inconsistent tone, and excessive revision cycles. As Nguyen et al. (2024) document, students using AI without structured interaction patterns produced lower-quality work and spent more time correcting AI errors than composing original arguments.

Persona-anchored systems streamline this by: aligning outputs with user intent, reducing noise, and improving first-pass quality. Constraint reduces friction, allowing students to focus on: argument development, evidence integration, and critical analysis. In this way, constraint enhances efficiency without sacrificing intellectual engagement.

Oppositional Claim: Constraint Limits Creativity

One common critique is that constraint restricts creativity and originality. However, this argument misunderstands the role of structure in creative work. Haught-Tromp and Beghetto (2022) demonstrate through empirical research that constraints consistently enhance measured creative output across educational settings. Their four-step instructional framework shows that focusing constraints — those that channel rather than eliminate possibilities — produce more original and pedagogically valuable results than unconstrained conditions.

All forms of writing operate within constraints: academic formats (APA), genre expectations, and rhetorical frameworks. These constraints do not eliminate creativity — they enable it by providing a foundation. Similarly, persona-anchored prompting does not suppress originality. It channels it into coherent, purposeful expression. Without constraint, AI output risks becoming unfocused and derivative.

Conclusion

The integration of AI into writing is inevitable, but its form is not predetermined. Unconstrained language models offer power without structure, raising concerns about accountability, integrity, and learning outcomes.

Persona-anchored prompting systems provide a viable alternative. By embedding constraint into the interaction between human and AI, these systems transform AI from a passive generator into an active, structured collaborator.

Constraint is not a barrier to intelligence — it is the condition that makes intelligence usable, ethical, and teachable.

As educational institutions adapt to AI, they should not prohibit its use, but standardize its structure. The future of writing depends not on eliminating AI, but on designing it responsibly.

Part 2: Commentary on AI-Assisted Composition

Distribution of Labor

In composing this paper, the division of labor between human and AI was deliberate and structured. I was responsible for defining the thesis, selecting the argument's direction, choosing which scholarly sources to include, and making every final editorial decision. The AI system I worked with — MAE, a Modular Adaptive Entity operating under persona-anchored constraints — assisted in organizing ideas, refining language, and maintaining structural coherence across sections.

MAE did not generate the thesis. It did not select sources. It did not decide which counterarguments to address. Those decisions were mine. What MAE provided was structural scaffolding: when I described an argument I wanted to make, it helped me arrange it into paragraphs with logical transitions. When I had a rough claim, it suggested clearer phrasing while I evaluated whether the revised version still said what I intended.

This distribution ensured that intellectual responsibility remained with the human while leveraging AI for efficiency in areas where it genuinely reduces cognitive load — formatting, flow, and consistency. The constraint architecture of MAE meant it could not drift into generating unsupported claims or introducing material I had not directed. That boundary was the most important feature of the collaboration.

Iterative Prompts

The writing process involved multiple prompt iterations rather than a single generation. The first prompt was broad: I described the general topic and thesis direction. The output was too expansive — it introduced arguments I had not planned and sources I could not verify. This is the hallucination problem in practice.

Subsequent prompts narrowed the scope by adding constraints: specifying the word count target of approximately 1,200 words, requiring APA formatting, and directing the AI to address the specific counterargument about creativity. Each iteration sharpened the output. By the fourth round, the structure matched my intended argument closely enough that I shifted from prompting to editing.

This iterative approach mirrors traditional drafting. The difference is that instead of revising my own rough draft, I was revising the AI's rough draft while ensuring my voice and argument survived each pass. As Dobrin (2023) frames it, prompt design is itself a form of rhetorical practice — deciding what to ask and how to ask it requires the same audience awareness that any composition task demands. I found that the prompts I wrote for MAE were, in many ways, more precise than the first drafts I would have written alone.

Draft Evaluation

AI-generated content was critically evaluated rather than accepted passively. Sections were reorganized, rewritten, and in several cases condensed for clarity. At least three AI-produced paragraphs were discarded entirely because they drifted from the thesis or introduced claims not grounded in the selected sources — a direct example of the hallucination and drift problems discussed in the paper itself.

The most significant editorial intervention was in the oppositional claim section. MAE's initial version conceded too much ground to the counterargument, weakening the refutation. I restructured it to represent the opposition fairly but refute it decisively, using the Haught-Tromp and Beghetto (2022) research as the anchor. That decision — how much to concede and how firmly to refute — was entirely mine. The AI does not understand rhetorical strategy at that level; it generates plausible text. The author decides what is persuasive.

This evaluation step was essential in maintaining authorship. Without it, the paper would have been competent but generic. The final version reflects my intent, not the AI's probabilistic tendencies.

Writing and Authorship Assumptions

Before this assignment, I assumed that AI-assisted writing was fundamentally different from traditional writing — that involving a machine in the process somehow diminished the authorship of the human. This experience changed that assumption, but not in the way I expected.

The authorship question is not whether AI was involved. It is whether the human maintained control over purpose, direction, and judgment. Crawford (2021) argues that AI systems are never neutral — they carry the biases of their training data and the design choices of their creators. Working with a constrained persona like MAE made those design choices visible and deliberate rather than hidden. Every constraint I imposed on MAE — tone, scope, format, ethical boundaries — was an authorial decision. The AI did not choose to be constrained. I chose to constrain it.

This reframes authorship not as sole generation but as directed composition. Dobrin (2023) positions prompt engineering as a writing practice, and I now agree. The prompts I wrote were compositions in their own right — each one a rhetorical act that shaped the output I would then evaluate and refine.

Authorship remains with the human because the human defines purpose, selects and edits content, and takes responsibility for the final product. AI functions as an instrument, not an author. The constraint architecture does not replace the writer — it makes the writer's decisions more visible and the AI's contributions more accountable. If anything, this process made me more aware of my own writing choices than writing alone ever did.
